SEO should start before website development, because keywords determine the site's structure, URLs and headings. If you only think about SEO after the website launches, part of the work has to be redone, and that takes extra time and money. In this article I explain how I start every project and why you should do the same.

The most common mistake I see with clients

Almost everyone who comes to me with a finished website admits the same thing: during development they did not know SEO even existed. The website was built as a business card, not as a tool that brings customers from Google.

That is why these sites often lack the very basics. The pages have no proper title and meta description — the texts Google shows in the search results above and below the link. If they are empty or the same on every page, it is harder for Google and for users to understand what each page is about.

These are not just technical details. Your entire visibility in search rests on these basics, and even a beautiful design will not replace them.

Why SEO should be planned before development, not after

A website's structure, URL addresses and heading hierarchy are set at the start of development. If they have to be changed later for SEO reasons, it takes time and creates risks.

I experienced this myself. I once changed a site's URL structure but did not notice that Google Ads was still sending people to the old addresses. For four days the ads sent people to a 404 page, and I lost that ad budget. I wrote about it in more detail in the article about my first SEO mistakes.

If the URLs and structure are well thought out from the start, changes like these are not needed later.

Keyword research is the first step

I start every project with keyword research, not with design. I use Google Keyword Planner to see which words and phrases your potential customers type into Google.

Once that is known, you can decide which pages the website needs. Each important service or product group usually has its own page with its own heading, URL and text. That way Google can link each page to specific searches.

After launch you can move on to deeper questions: technical optimisation, content quality and backlinks. But if the basics are put together correctly, everything else is much easier.

Example: video in the hero section and loading screens

One of the most common problems I see is a video in the top part of the page, the hero section. It looks impressive, but it often makes the page slow. If a page loads slowly, some visitors leave before they have seen your offer.

Even worse are purpose-built loading screens meant to make the site seem more impressive. For the user they just mean waiting. That is why, in development, I first think about how quickly a person gets to the content, and only then about effects.

Google measures page speed and usability with Core Web Vitals metrics. You can check them in Google Search Console and PageSpeed Insights.

What I build into every website

When I develop a website, the SEO basics are included from day one:

  • technically clean code and fast loading, with Core Web Vitals in mind;
  • a mobile version that is easy to use on a phone;
  • a clear URL structure that matches the page content;
  • a correct heading hierarchy — one H1 per page and logical H2s and H3s below it;
  • an SSL certificate, so the site opens over a secure https connection.

This does not mean the website will immediately be on the first page of Google. It means it will have no technical obstacles that prevent it from appearing in the search results.

SEO and advertising: the difference

With Google Ads you pay for every click, and as soon as the budget runs out, the ad disappears. SEO works differently. A page that is well structured and answers people's searches can keep receiving organic visits even when you are no longer actively paying for it.

Rankings are not permanent, though, because competitors and Google's algorithms change. That is why I see SEO as a long-term investment that needs to be checked from time to time.
